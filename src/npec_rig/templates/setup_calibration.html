How this number is calculated, and what it means

Every calibration tag's real size is measured with callipers, not taken from the packaging (the single biggest source of error found in this project's history — a "30 mm" tag actually measured 29.3106 mm, a ~4.7% error in area if trusted). From that measured size and the tag's four detected corners, each tag gives a real, measured scale (pixels per millimetre) at its own spot in the photo.

Those measured scales are fitted to a single tilted plane (a camera looking down at an angle sees things nearer the lens as "bigger per millimetre" than things farther away — a plane is the simplest shape that captures that). The percentage shown at any point on the photo is not that fit directly: it is how far off the fit was for the 5 calibration tags physically nearest that point, combined so that tags reading slightly big and slightly small don't cancel each other out to zero — an even split either way still counts as real disagreement.

What it means: a direct, measured estimate of how far a canopy-area reading at that spot could be off, based on how consistent the real calibration tags near it actually were — not a theoretical lens or camera error estimate. Blue genuinely means "several nearby tags agreed closely." Red usually means thin coverage nearby (see the coverage lines above), not a fault in the camera or the maths.

Caveat: "5 nearest tags" has no distance limit. A spot with no real coverage anywhere near it still gets a colour, borrowed from whichever tags happen to be closest in the photo, however far that really is. Blue in a completely uncovered area (e.g. off the edge of the table) means "the nearest real measurements happened to agree," not "this spot was actually measured."

How this number is calculated, and what it means

The scale field above only ever measures length (pixels per millimetre in one direction). Turning that into an area conversion — needed to report canopy area in mm², not pixels — means squaring it, which is only exactly right if every tag's photographed shape is a perfect square. Near a tilted table's edges, a real square can photograph as a slightly non-square quadrilateral (a "keystone" shape), and squaring the length scale would silently miss that.

Rather than model the lens or the table's tilt angle to predict this — the kind of camera maths this project has failed at three times before, see CLAUDE.md — it's measured directly: each tag's real pixel area, computed exactly from its four detected corners (a standard geometry formula, no lens model, no assumptions), compared to what a perfect square with that tag's own average side length would cover. A square is the largest-area shape possible for a given average side length, so this comparison can only ever come out as zero (a genuine square) or negative (some real distortion) — never positive; that's also why this heatmap's colour scale only runs one direction.

What it means: answers "is squaring the length scale above good enough for area, or does area need its own correction before the canopy-area feature (M5) relies on it?" Flat/near-zero everywhere means squaring is fine. Measured across 17 real calibration runs on this rig (2026-09-21): the worst tag anywhere came out at −0.46%, and the pattern gets worse toward the table's corners in a way that tracks position, not tag size or image noise (checked separately, by comparing against tag size directly across 2104 tags) — so it reads as a real, if very small, geometric effect, not noise. Logged as an open scope decision in TODO.md, not acted on further without sign-off, since an area-specific fit is exactly the calibration maths CLAUDE.md's evidence gates exist to hold off on.

Caveat: this heatmap uses a much narrower colour scale (0–0.5%) than the confidence heatmap above (0–8%), stretched to fill the same blue-to-red range either way. The two images can look equally "hot" by eye at completely different real magnitudes — only the printed percentages say which is actually bigger. The two numbers also measure different things and aren't copies of each other: on a given run they've come out statistically uncorrelated, even though both tend to look worse toward the table's edges — for unrelated reasons (coverage gaps here vs. real geometry above).

Is this lens/parallax distortion, or something else?

PLAN.md's own rule: a lens/parallax distortion signature is structured — residual trends with distance from the image centre, with a consistent sign — not random scatter. That's the specific measurement that decides whether picking up lens-distortion modelling is justified, so it's plotted directly rather than guessed at: every tag's own signed residual (not folded to a percentage magnitude — a real distortion trend has a sign) against its distance from the image centre, with an unweighted linear fit and its R². No judgement is baked in here about whether a given R² counts as "structured" — read the scatter and the fit yourself.

Dome of certainty

Claimed uncertainty (already computed and saved by every run) sampled at each calibration tag's own position, plotted against distance from the image centre, with an unweighted linear fit and its R². Certainty should be highest at the centre and fall toward the edges — a "dome" shape. This shows whether the claimed number actually behaves that way, or is flat/noisy instead.
